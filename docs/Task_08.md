# Task 8: Iterative Methods for Linear Systems

## 1. Inputs and Mathematical Operations

In this task, I used the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods to solve systems of linear equations. Both methods start with an initial guess and keep updating the values until the difference between one iteration and the next is smaller than the set tolerance.

The two methods differ in how they use the calculated values. Jacobi uses the values from the previous iteration, while Gauss-Seidel uses each new value immediately as it is calculated. I used NumPy to create the coefficient matrix and calculate a direct solution for comparison. Matplotlib was used to display the convergence graphs.

## 2. Parameter Change and Results

In the original program, I set the left boundary temperature to 100°C and the right boundary temperature to 20°C. The program used five interior points to calculate the temperatures along the rod.

The original program produced the following results:

- Jacobi temperatures: `[86.67, 73.33, 60.00, 46.67, 33.33]`
- Gauss-Seidel temperatures: `[86.67, 73.33, 60.00, 46.67, 33.33]`
- Direct solution: `[86.67, 73.33, 60.00, 46.67, 33.33]`
- Jacobi iterations: 150.

To see how changing an input would affect the results, I increased the right boundary temperature from 20°C to 60°C and kept the left boundary at 100°C.

The modified program produced the following results:

- Jacobi temperatures: `[93.33, 86.67, 80.00, 73.33, 66.67]`

As expected, increasing the temperature at the right end caused the temperatures at all five interior points to increase. I also noticed that Gauss-Seidel used fewer iterations than Jacobi in both cases.

## 3. Explanation of Output

Both methods successfully calculated the temperatures along the rod. Their results were almost the same as the direct solution obtained using NumPy. This shows that the two methods were able to find accurate solutions.

The convergence graphs show how the maximum change in the calculated values becomes smaller as the number of iterations increases. This means that the results are getting closer to a stable solution.

From the results, Gauss-Seidel reached the required accuracy in fewer iterations than Jacobi. This shows that it can be more efficient than Jacobi for this example.

## 4. Related Real-World Case: Heat Distribution in a Metal Rod

For the real-world application, I used the methods to estimate how temperature is distributed along a metal rod. I divided the rod into 20 interior points, with the left end kept at 0°C and the right end at 80°C.

### Assumptions

- The temperatures at both ends of the rod remain constant.
- The rod has reached a steady state.
- There is no heat loss through the sides of the rod.
- The temperature is calculated at 20 interior points.

### Inputs and Outputs

I used NumPy to create the system of linear equations representing the temperatures along the rod. I then used Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel to calculate the temperatures and compared their results with the direct solution obtained using NumPy.

The program produced the following results:

- Jacobi iterations: 1,243.
- Gauss-Seidel iterations: 627.
- Maximum error between Jacobi and direct solution: `0.00004406`.
- Maximum error between Gauss-Seidel and direct solution: `0.00004357`.
- Average temperature at the middle of the rod: `40.000°C`.

### Interpretation

The results show that both methods gave temperatures very close to the direct solution. The errors were very small, meaning that the calculated temperatures were accurate. Gauss-Seidel also reached the required accuracy in fewer iterations than Jacobi.

The average temperature at the middle of the rod was 40°C, which is between the temperatures at the two ends. This makes sense because the temperature changes gradually from 0°C at one end to 80°C at the other.

This practical example helped me understand how the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods can be used to calculate temperature distribution in a metal rod. It also showed me that Gauss-Seidel required fewer iterations than Jacobi in this case.
